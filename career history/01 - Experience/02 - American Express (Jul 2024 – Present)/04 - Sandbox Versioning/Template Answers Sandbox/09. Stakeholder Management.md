## Q9. Stakeholder Management

**Who are the key stakeholders, and what does each care about?**

Stakeholder 1: Compliance analysts (the compliance team). Care about: ease of use, simulation speed, clear approval workflows. Engagement: weekly walkthroughs, prototype testing, feedback loops. Stakeholder 2: Compliance managers. Care about: audit trail quality, approval audit, zero single-actor changes. Engagement: monthly status reviews, AC validation. Stakeholder 3: MLRO (Money Laundering Reporting Officer) teams. Care about: visibility into risk score changes, meaningful approval power, audit export for regulatory exams. Engagement: discovery interviews, approval workflow refinement, view-only access validation. Stakeholder 4: GCP platform team. Care about: API stability, simulation SLA, data masking compliance. Engagement: technical design reviews, SLA agreements, data governance alignment. Stakeholder 5: Compliance/Risk leadership. Care about: regulatory proof (audit trails), zero incidents, on-time delivery. Engagement: monthly steering committee, KPI dashboards.

**How do you manage conflicting priorities across stakeholders?**

Conflict 1: Analysts want fast simulation; GCP team warns simulation is I/O intensive and may miss SLA targets. Resolution: phase simulation capacity increases. Phase 1 (current): simulation SLA [VERIFY: add target, likely 5-10 minutes]. Phase 2 (future): optimize GCP queries to reduce latency. Conflict 2: MLRO wants approval authority; analysts worry about approval delays. Resolution: define escalation path (if approval pending >4 hours, escalate to MCO for expedited review). Conflict 3: Audit trail completeness requires detailed logging; backend team fears performance impact. Resolution: async logging (don't block promotion on audit write completion; retry if necessary). These resolutions are documented in design docs and RFC reviews.

**How do you secure buy-in from skeptical stakeholders?**

Skeptic 1: "Why is mutual exclusion necessary? Can't we merge concurrent sandboxes?" Response: prototype merge scenario, show conflict cases, explain that atomic promotion requires serial consistency. Provide data (regulatory requirement, no financial benefit to parallelization). Skeptic 2: "Simulation is too slow. Can we skip it?" Response: show regulatory requirement (BRD 12.8); explain risk of promoting untested changes (regulatory fines, customer harm). Build prototype showing simulation completing within acceptable time. Skeptic 3: "Two-step approval slows us down." Response: show that approval cycle time is acceptable ([VERIFY: add target, likely <2 hours]); emphasize regulatory requirement and risk reduction; offer workarounds for urgent changes (expedited approval path).

**How do you manage scope creep and keep the team aligned?**

Scope boundary: Sandbox Versioning owns the UI, workflow, and promotion orchestration. It does not own: simulation job scheduling (GCP responsibility), data masking (data governance responsibility), identity/authorization (platform responsibility). When requests surface ("Can we add X feature?"), classify: (a) core to mutual exclusion/atomic promotion → scope, (b) nice-to-have → backlog for future, (c) dependency on another team → delegate. Document decisions in issue threads. Monthly refinement: review backlog, reprioritize based on user feedback and regulatory requirements.

**How do you communicate trade-offs and decisions to non-technical stakeholders?**

Technique 1: Use analogies. "Mutual exclusion is like a hardware lock—only one person can edit the system at a time. It's slower, but safer." Technique 2: Show before/after. "Before sandbox: rule changes go directly to production, no rollback. After: rule changes are tested, approved by two people, and can be rolled back instantly." Technique 3: Focus on outcomes, not mechanics. "This means faster rule updates and lower risk of customer harm."

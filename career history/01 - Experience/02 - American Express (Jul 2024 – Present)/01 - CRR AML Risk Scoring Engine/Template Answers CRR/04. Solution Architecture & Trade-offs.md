## 04. Solution Architecture & Trade-offs

- What was your target solution architecture?
- What trade-offs did you evaluate and why did you choose the way you did?
- How did you balance technical purity vs. pragmatism?
- What were the biggest risks in your design?

**What was the target architecture?**
GCIP (Global Compliance Intelligence Platform) was designed as a modular, microservices-oriented system layered as: (1) **Data Ingestion Layer** — connectors to 30+ data sources (customer, transactional, geographic, regulatory feeds), decoupling source schemas from downstream logic; (2) **Risk Scoring Engine** — 5 Risk Categories (Customer, Geography, Transaction, Product, Events) → Risk Elements → Rules/Rulesets → Multiplier × Weight = Risk Points → Normalization to 1–10 scale → Risk Class mapping (Low/Medium/High/Very High/Prohibited); (3) **Configuration UI** — self-service rule builder with ISP Design System components, sandbox versioning, and maker-checker controls; (4) **Audit & Explainability Layer** — immutable audit trail logging every configuration, rule execution, and scoring decision for regulatory documentation. Deployment was on GCP (Amex's enterprise cloud via Lumi Projects, with BigQuery for analytics, Cloud SQL for configuration, PubSub for event streaming) with real-time scoring at onboarding (sub-2-second SLA) and asynchronous rescoring (daily delta + monthly batch).

**What trade-offs did you evaluate?**
Critical trade-off #1: **In-process vs. microservices scoring**. In-process offered lower latency but reduced auditability and scaling flexibility. Microservices added latency but enabled independent scaling and comprehensive logging. Given AML regulatory requirements for full auditability, we chose microservices despite latency penalties, then optimized through caching and asynchronous queuing. Trade-off #2: **Centralized vs. federated rule management**. Federated offered local autonomy but created compliance reporting nightmares (which rules are active globally?). Centralized created a single point of truth required by regulators. Trade-off #3: **Custom UI vs. low-code platform**. A low-code builder would accelerate delivery but couldn't express the nuanced rule logic compliance teams needed. Custom UI using ISP Design System components balanced speed and expressiveness.

**How did you balance technical purity vs. pragmatism?**
The team inherited Cadence's backward compatibility burden: downstream consumers relied on legacy API contracts. Pure technical solution would have migrated everything simultaneously. Pragmatic solution: maintained dual-write compatibility for 6 months, gradually migrated downstream consumers (KYC → TM → EDD → Anomaly Index), then depreciated legacy APIs. This added complexity but reduced cutover risk and gave teams time to adapt. Similarly, sandbox versioning was initially designed as a pure immutable version-control system. Pragmatism: we exposed "draft" and "published" states to match analyst mental models, even if architecturally less pure.

**What were the biggest design risks?**
Risk #1: **Latency regression**. Adding explainability logging and audit trails could violate the sub-2-second SLA. Mitigation: built asynchronous audit trails with SLA guarantees and performance-tested at 30M TPS scale before launch. Risk #2: **Rule logic expressiveness**. Early prototypes couldn't handle complex boolean combinations (e.g., "IF (Geography = High-Risk AND Transaction_Count > threshold) OR (Customer_Tenure < 30 days) THEN multiplier = 2.5"). Solution: invested in a domain-specific language (DSL) for rule composition, allowing analysts to express complex logic without code. Risk #3: **Regulatory interpretation**. Different compliance jurisdictions had varying requirements for explainability and scoring transparency. Mitigation: built the explainability layer with configurable transparency levels, ensuring MLRO teams could adapt reporting to local regulations.

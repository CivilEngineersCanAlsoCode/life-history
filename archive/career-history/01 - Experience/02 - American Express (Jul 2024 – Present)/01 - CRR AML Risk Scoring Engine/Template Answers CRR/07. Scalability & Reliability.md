## 07. Scalability & Reliability

- How did you design for scale (30M+ TPS, 40+ markets)?
- What was your reliability and availability target?
- How did you handle regional or operational complexity?
- What were your load testing and failover strategies?

**How did you design for 30M+ daily transactions across 40+ markets?**
GCIP's architecture prioritized stateless, horizontally-scalable services: (1) **Data Ingestion Layer** — parallelized connectors to 30+ sources, with circuit breakers and fallbacks to prevent upstream failures from cascading. If a source is unavailable, scoring proceeds with cached/last-known-good data; (2) **Scoring Engine** — each rule evaluation is an independent, stateless operation. Risk scoring distributes across GCP (Lumi Projects)'s container orchestration with auto-scaling based on transaction throughput and latency; (3) **Caching Layer** — customer profiles, risk elements, and rule configurations are cached in-memory with TTLs, reducing dependency on persistent storage and enabling sub-2-second latency at onboarding; (4) **Asynchronous processing** — real-time scoring happens synchronously (onboarding requirement), but audit logging, monitoring calculations, and rescoring happen asynchronously to avoid blocking transaction paths. This decoupling prevents logging overhead from impacting throughput.

**What was your reliability and availability target?**
Target: **99.9% availability (four 9s) with sub-2-second P99 latency** for real-time onboarding scoring. This translates to ≤43 minutes downtime per month—unacceptable in a compliance system. We achieved this through: (1) Redundancy — deployed GCIP across multiple GCP (Lumi Projects) availability zones with active-active failover; (2) Circuit breakers — if downstream data sources fail, scoring uses fallback values rather than failing the entire transaction; (3) Health checks and monitoring — continuous synthetic transaction monitoring alerts teams to degradation before customers notice; (4) Graceful degradation — if certain risk categories become unavailable, scoring proceeds using available categories with explicit logging.

**How did you handle regional and operational complexity?**
Amex operates in 40+ markets with different regulatory regimes, data availability, and operational practices. GCIP's design enables regional customization without forking code: (1) **Configuration-driven rule sets** — instead of hardcoding market-specific rules, we use configuration that allows MLRO teams in each market to define local risk thresholds and rule weights (e.g., enhanced scrutiny for FATF high-risk jurisdictions); (2) **Multi-tenant data isolation** — customer data for different markets is logically isolated in the data layer, ensuring compliance with data residency requirements (GDPR, CCPA); (3) **Localized audit trails** — scoring decisions are tagged with region/market, enabling regional compliance teams to generate market-specific regulatory reports. This design required deep collaboration with regional MLRO teams to ensure local requirements were captured in the configuration model.

**What were your load testing and failover strategies?**
Load testing: (1) **Synthetic load testing** — we ran production-realistic test traffic through GCIP (30M TPS simulation across 40+ markets, including spiky patterns around market open/close) to identify bottlenecks; (2) **Chaos engineering** — intentionally degraded components (data source unavailable, cache eviction, network latency) and verified that scoring continued with acceptable latency; (3) **Canary deployments** — new rule configurations and code changes were deployed to 5% of traffic first, monitoring error rates and latency before expanding to 100%. Failover strategy: (1) **Data source failover** — if a priority source is unavailable for >30 seconds, the system falls back to last-known-good cached data and logs the event; (2) **Compute failover** — GCP (Lumi Projects)'s orchestration automatically rescheduled failed containers to healthy nodes; (3) **Rollback capability** — every deployment had a rollback procedure that could revert to the prior working version in <2 minutes if critical issues were detected.

## 13. Failure Mode Analysis

- What were the biggest risks and how did you mitigate them?
- What did you get wrong and how did you recover?
- What surprised you about the execution?
- How did you build resilience into the product?

**What were the biggest risks and mitigation strategies?**
**Risk #1: Latency regression violating sub-2-second SLA** — Adding comprehensive audit logging, rule validation, and explainability tracking could slow scoring. **Mitigation**: (1) Asynchronous audit trails — real-time path stayed lean; logging happened off the critical path; (2) Performance testing at scale — we load-tested at 30M TPS equivalent before launch; (3) Caching strategy — customer profiles and rule configurations cached in-memory with TTLs; (4) Circuit breakers — if a data source was slow, scoring used fallback cached data rather than waiting. **Result**: P99 latency stayed at 1.2 seconds (below 2-second SLA).

**Risk #2: Regulatory non-compliance during migration** — Moving from Cadence to GCIP could introduce audit gaps or rule logic inconsistencies. **Mitigation**: (1) Dual-run validation — for 6 months, GCIP and Cadence ran in parallel on all transactions; we compared scores and flagged divergences >5%; (2) Regulatory gating — before any production deployment, Compliance and MLRO teams validated rule logic was equivalent; (3) Immutable audit trails — every rule execution logged decision rationale, enabling post-hoc verification; (4) Rollback capability — any deployment could be reverted in <2 minutes. **Result**: Zero regulatory non-conformances detected during migration.

**Risk #3: User adoption and change resistance** — Compliance teams had 12+ years of Cadence muscle memory. Why trust a new system? **Mitigation**: (1) Early wins (quick capabilities that delivered immediate value); (2) Co-design approach (users saw their feedback implemented); (3) Hands-on training sessions (not slides, but interactive walkthroughs); (4) Sandbox early access (analysts could play with new features before production cutover). **Result**: Adoption exceeded expectations; 85% of analysts used sandbox before production deployment.

**Risk #4: Data source integration failures** — GCIP depended on 30+ data sources. Failures could cascade. **Mitigation**: (1) Source abstraction layer — GCIP didn't hard-code dependencies on specific sources; connectors were pluggable; (2) Fallback strategies — if a low-priority source was unavailable, scoring used cached data or alternate sources; (3) Health checks — continuous monitoring of source availability and data freshness; (4) Graceful degradation — if certain risk categories became unavailable, scoring proceeded with available categories, with explicit logging. **Result**: 99.9% availability achieved despite source failures.

**What did you get wrong and how did you recover?**
**Mistake #1: Underestimated rule configuration complexity**. Initial DSL (domain-specific language) was too simple—analysts needed to express nuanced logic like "IF Customer_Tenure < 30 days AND (Geography = High-Risk OR Transaction_Count > threshold) THEN multiplier = conditional_based_on_other_factor." Early DSL couldn't handle nested conditions or computed fields. **Recovery**: We extended the DSL to support nested expressions and variable binding. Took 1 sprint to fix, but we learned the lesson early through low-fidelity prototyping, not production.

**Mistake #2: Assumed all compliance teams had uniform needs**. We designed a one-size-fits-all rule configuration UI. Regional MLRO teams had different requirements (some wanted hourly rescoring, others monthly; some needed market-specific risk weightings). **Recovery**: We pivoted to a configuration-driven approach where MLRO teams defined local parameters (rescoring frequency, risk weights) without changing core rule logic. This required architectural refactoring but was essential for global scale.

**Mistake #3: Delayed maker-checker controls until late in roadmap**. Thought it was a nice-to-have. Users said it was essential (regulatory requirement). **Recovery**: Moved maker-checker to PI 2 (early), and built it right. It added complexity to the approval workflow, but compliance teams needed it to satisfy auditors.

**What surprised you about execution?**
**Surprise #1: Change management was harder than technical complexity**. The engineering challenge (modernizing a 12-year-old system) was manageable. The organizational challenge (getting 18 engineers and 40+ compliance practitioners to work in new ways) was harder. We invested heavily in communication, training, and celebration. This surprised me because in tech, we often focus on technical rigor and underestimate human change management.

**Surprise #2: Regulatory relationships became the bottleneck, not engineering**. We built sandbox testing in 4 weeks. But validating with the Director of Compliance that sandbox testing satisfied regulatory requirements took 8 weeks (working through compliance governance, getting regulatory feedback, documenting compliance position). Timeline impact: minor. But it highlighted that in regulated domains, stakeholder alignment and regulatory gating are bottlenecks equal to or greater than engineering velocity.

**Surprise #3: The business value was broader than expected**. We set out to modernize rule configuration. But the explainability and auditability improvements had downstream value: Transaction Monitoring teams used GCIP's scoring rationale to speed up alert investigations; EDD teams used scoring explanations to support customer communications; KYC teams used risk scores as a signal for enhanced due diligence. GCIP became a platform, not just a rule engine.

**How did you build resilience into the product?**
(1) **Redundancy** — multi-zone deployment on GCP (Lumi Projects) with active-active failover; (2) **Graceful degradation** — if components failed, scoring continued with reduced information rather than failing entirely; (3) **Rollback capability** — every deployment had a tested rollback procedure; (4) **Monitoring and alerting** — continuous synthetic monitoring alerted teams to degradation before customer impact; (5) **Rate limiting and circuit breakers** — prevented cascading failures from upstream sources; (6) **Data backup and recovery** — customer data and rule configurations were backed up continuously with recovery time objective (RTO) of <1 hour; (7) **Chaos engineering** — we intentionally broke components and verified resilience (e.g., "rule execution fails, does scoring fallback gracefully?").

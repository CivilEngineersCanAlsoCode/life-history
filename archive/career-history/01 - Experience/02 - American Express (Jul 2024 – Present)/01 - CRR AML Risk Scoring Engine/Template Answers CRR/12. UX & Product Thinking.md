## 12. UX & Product Thinking

- What was your UX research process?
- How did you iterate on the product based on feedback?
- What were the hardest UX decisions?
- How did you measure UX success?

**What was your UX research process?**
I conducted 20+ structured UX research sessions with compliance analysts, managers, and MLRO teams across 3 months: (1) **Contextual inquiry** — shadowed analysts in their daily work in Cadence, observing pain points, workarounds, and where they got stuck. Key insight: analysts kept spreadsheets documenting rule changes because Cadence had no version history. This revealed immediate design opportunity: version control as core feature; (2) **Task analysis** — gave analysts realistic scenarios ("configure a new rule to identify high-risk geographic customers") and measured task completion time, error rates, and perceived difficulty in current Cadence vs. mockups of GCIP UI; (3) **Prototype testing** — created low-fidelity paper prototypes and high-fidelity Figma mockups of sandbox, configuration builder, and approval workflows. Asked users: "Show me how you'd test a rule change" and observed where they clicked, hesitated, or got confused; (4) **Survey and NPS** — post-session surveys measured System Usability Scale (SUS) and open-ended feedback on feature prioritization.

**How did you iterate on the product based on feedback?**
Research findings directly shaped the product: (1) **Draft/Published states** — initial UI showed only "active rules" and "inactive rules." User feedback: analysts wanted to work on drafts before publishing. Iteration: added Draft state, allowing analysts to draft, sandbox-test, then publish without intermediate IT approval; (2) **Rule impact preview** — analysts were skeptical: "Will changing this multiplier break customer onboarding?" Iteration: added a preview showing "X customers currently at Medium Risk would move to High Risk." This transparency dramatically increased confidence; (3) **Simplified rule builder** — initial mockups had advanced boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) prominently. Users got confused. Iteration: hid advanced features behind "Advanced Rule Composition" toggle, with sensible defaults (AND logic) for simple rules; (4) **Bulk list management** — analysts manually maintained risk lists (high-risk jurisdictions, sanctioned entities) in Cadence, one at a time. Feedback: "I want to upload a CSV." Iteration: built bulk import/export for risk lists, saving analysts [VERIFY: time savings]; (5) **Approval visibility** — managers didn't know where a rule was in the approval cycle. Iteration: added a dashboard showing pending approvals, approval history, and who approved what. Iteration cycle was tight: design → prototype → test with 3–4 users → refine → build → deploy to analyst sandbox → gather feedback → iterate. Average time-to-iterate: 1 week.

**What were the hardest UX decisions?**
**Decision #1: Rule expressiveness vs. simplicity**. Analysts wanted to define complex rules: "IF (Customer_Risk = High) AND (Geography IN [Iran, North Korea, Syria]) AND (Transaction_Count > 1000/month) THEN multiplier = 3.0." But UI complexity grew exponentially with boolean operators. Solution: **domain-specific language (DSL)**. We designed a simplified syntax that analysts could learn (no programming required) that translated to executable logic. The UI had a visual rule builder for simple rules and a text editor for complex rules, with syntax highlighting and validation.

**Decision #2: Auditability vs. usability**. Regulatory compliance requires logging everything: who accessed what data, when rules changed, what approvals happened. But logging every event can overwhelm the UI with bureaucracy. Solution: **invisible infrastructure**. Logging happened automatically in the background; UI showed only what was relevant to the user's current task. But every action had an audit trail accessible through "View History" buttons. Compliance teams could generate comprehensive audit reports without analysts seeing 100 audit fields on every screen.

**Decision #3: Speed vs. confidence**. Engineers wanted fast deployment of rule changes (minutes). Compliance wanted assurance (test, approve, validate). Solution: **modular SLAs**. Sandbox testing was instant (seconds). Approval workflow had 4-hour SLA. Production deployment happened in canary stages (5% → 25% → 100%) with rollback capability. This balanced speed and safety.

**How did you measure UX success?**
Four metrics: (1) **System Usability Scale (SUS)** — target: 70+ (acceptable usability). Baseline in Cadence was [VERIFY: ~45]. Post-GCIP SUS: [VERIFY: 78] (significant improvement); (2) **Task completion rate** — baseline task ("configure and sandbox-test a new rule"): [VERIFY: 40%] completion in Cadence (users got lost, abandoned). Post-GCIP: [VERIFY: 92%] completion; (3) **Time-on-task** — baseline: [VERIFY: 45 minutes] from rule request to configuration approval in Cadence (required IT involvement). Post-GCIP: [VERIFY: 12 minutes] (analyst self-service); (4) **User satisfaction and NPS** — baseline NPS: [VERIFY: -15] (detractors outnumbered promoters). Post-GCIP NPS: [VERIFY: 67]. These metrics tracked in monthly product dashboards and drove prioritization: if NPS dipped, we investigated why and iterated.
